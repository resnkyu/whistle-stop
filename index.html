The problem

Public procurement is where public money meets private suppliers, which makes it a natural place to look for corruption risk. This project asks how far AI-assisted analysis of the public record can help.

The research question

To what extent can AI-assisted analysis of public procurement data improve the identification of corruption risks and institutional accountability in Kazakhstan?

The question inside the issue

The central argument is that corruption is not simply a problem of missing information. It is also a problem of institutional incentives, weak oversight and the willingness to act on evidence that is already available. The theoretical foundation combines principal–agent theory and institutional theory.

The project does not try to address corruption in every form. It concentrates on suspicious procurement patterns that show up in public tender information, contract records, supplier data and procurement prices.

Evidence on the challenges of procurement oversight comes from the National Anti-Corruption Report for 2024, which documents inflated prices, unjustified single-source purchases and weak planning, and from academic work by Junusbekova and Khamitov (2021) and Khamitov, Knox and Junusbekova (2023). The institutional ground is also moving: the Anti-Corruption Agency that wrote that report was dissolved on 30 June 2025 and its functions were transferred to other bodies, including the National Security Committee (OECD, 2026). Whoever receives a flag tomorrow may not be who received it yesterday.

The data, and its silences

The raw material is Kazakhstan’s official Public Procurement Portal and its API. The project examines procurement methods, contract amounts, supplier patterns and competition indicators.

Open contracting data on general procurement is published on the government portal (goszakup.gov.kz) and can be analysed through its public API, according to the Open Contracting Partnership. The portal’s developer documentation is at procurement.gov.kz/ru/developer/ows_v2. Civil society already works with this data: the Open Contracting Partnership reports that the Adildik Joly project (2022–2024) identified more than 1,000 cases of inflated prices worth 15.1 billion tenge, leading to 98 criminal cases. The 2024 National Report states that the state’s own Unified Procurement Window prevented overpricing of more than 55 billion tenge, of which 26 billion was returned to the budget. These are reported figures from the organisations involved.

The chart plots three published CPI readings. The four indicators below are specified, not yet run on portal records, so their error rates are unknown. That is a limit of this version, and the verdict takes it into account.

Four indicators

Repeated single bidders

How often an institution’s tenders receive only one bid. Other readings: an urgent purchase, a niche supplier or a thin market.

Price anomalies

Contract prices that sit far from comparable purchases. Other readings: different specifications, quantities or delivery terms.

Supplier concentration

How much of an institution’s spending goes to a few suppliers. Other readings: a specialised market with few providers.

Unusual patterns

Other irregularities in procedure, timing or amounts. Indicators are adjusted to the procurement context.

What the data cannot say

Underreporting

Many cases never reach the records that statistics are built from.

Inaccessible information

Some information is not published, or not in a usable form.

Inconsistent records

Records differ in format and completeness. The Open Contracting Partnership notes that four government sources report differing figures, and that data on state-owned enterprises and on mining, oil and gas procurement is harder to access.

Perception is not cases

The index tracks how corruption is perceived, not how often it happens.

Official statistics do not capture every instance of corruption. The project therefore avoids unsupported statistics: a figure is used only if its original source was checked, and claims that could not be traced to a source were left out.

What this adds up to

Together these works cover Kazakhstan’s procurement environment, the limits of AI-assisted research, human overreliance on automated recommendations, environmental costs and responsible AI governance. What is settled: procurement is a high-risk area in Kazakhstan, and AI output is only as good as the data and the people who check it. What is contested: whether better monitoring changes behaviour at all. Principal–agent reforms assume someone honest at the top to act on a flag (Persson, Rothstein and Teorell, 2013), and institutions shape what actors dare to do with information (North, 1990).

The gap: among these sources, none tests AI-assisted risk flagging on Kazakhstan’s procurement records together with human verification, a right of reply and an explicit accounting of costs. This project speaks to that gap, as a design to be argued rather than a tool that has been proven.

The AI-assisted solution

An AI-assisted look at public procurement records. An alert is never proof of corruption.

AI flags. Humans verify.
  • The tool analyses publicly available procurement records only. It collects no confidential whistleblower submissions and no personal allegations, and the first version has no public submission feature.
  • A Palace is an institution, never an individual, because corruption can become embedded in structures, procedures and incentives.
  • Indicators are explainable and link back to the original records.
  • Human auditors verify every alert and let affected institutions or parties explain and challenge it.
  • Indicators adjust to the procurement context.

ALICE was developed by Brazil’s Office of the Comptroller General (CGU). It analyses procurement documents and helps auditors identify potentially irregular transactions, and human auditors act on its alerts. These 2024 results are self-reported by the Brazilian government and do not mean that every alert was corruption.

Case study: Ukraine’s DOZORRO

0monitoring requests sent, 2016–2023
0criminal proceedings opened
0studies published

DOZORRO is run by Transparency International Ukraine on top of the open Prozorro e-procurement data. It uses a public set of risk indicators (40 in a 2019 description) to prioritise tenders for review by civil-society monitors, and it was an early tester of machine learning for risk detection. These figures are the organisation’s own, they count activity rather than corruption avoided, and its machine-learning work was only described as tested.

Both cases point the same way: the technology widens what humans can look at, and the results depend on people who act on the alerts.

The bill

Five costs. The bill is mostly human rather than computational. Where no checkable figure exists, this page says so instead of inventing one.

Environmental

The footprint should be kept small by prioritising conventional statistical analysis, using smaller models for textual interpretation, processing in batches and hosting efficiently, instead of running large language models continuously. The reference points are Luccioni et al. (2024) and Li et al. (2023). The grid matters: coal-fired plants produced 74.9% of Kazakhstan’s electricity in 2024 according to the system operator, and a more recent Energy Ministry figure is 62.1%, while renewables excluding large hydro were 6.2% (2024, Bureau of National Statistics). No energy figure is claimed because none has been measured; a pilot would have to meter it.

Economic

The project would ideally receive transparent public funding, with independent oversight of how it is implemented and how it performs. Payer: the state budget, since the data and the oversight duty are public. Likely beneficiaries: taxpayers and honest suppliers. Risk: a private vendor could end up owning the method. No tenge figure is given, because the main cost, auditor time plus data engineering, cannot be priced without a pilot. Benchmarks exist but are self-reported: Brazil credits ALICE with R$257.25 million in savings in 2024.

Labor

Human auditors must examine the original records, verify findings and hear from the affected institution. That work changes what auditors do and has to be resourced. The system also leans on hidden labour: data cleaning, labelling any text that language models read, and maintenance. Lin and Zhang (2025) show that model annotations must be validated by people before they can be trusted. The pilot metric that matters is the share of alerts auditors confirm per hour of auditor time.

Political and governance

Law No. 230-VIII “On Artificial Intelligence” (17 November 2025) is the main frame. It sets minimal, medium and high risk tiers, which the system’s owner or operator assigns, and three autonomy levels. Whistle Stop is designed as a low-autonomy system, where a person always makes the final choice. Its risk tier is a judgement: it fits medium risk, because a failure could cause moral harm or material damage, and a formal legal assessment would settle it. The law requires continuous human control of AI systems (Art. 8) and data protection (Art. 10). Buribayeva et al. (2026) warn that legal design, centralised data and weak accountability can matter more than model accuracy. Civil society’s room to act as an independent reviewer is also under pressure: since 2023 the Finance Ministry has published a register of more than 200 entities and individuals receiving foreign funding, which Transparency International criticised. Safeguards against political misuse are therefore a condition, not an extra.

Data ethics

The first version uses public records only and avoids unnecessary personal information. Data should be stored as Kazakhstan’s data-protection and localisation rules require, under the Law on Personal Data and Their Protection. Article 419 of the Criminal Code covers knowingly false denunciation, and a related offence, knowingly disseminating false information, is in Article 274. Criminal defamation was removed in 2020, though “insult” remains an offence. This is why alerts are about institutions and records, never named individuals.

Verdict

As a standalone fix: no. As a supporting instrument: only under strict conditions.

The six conditions

1. Meaningful human oversight

Auditors examine the original records before anything is acted on.

2. Explainable indicators

Every alert shows which indicator fired and why.

3. Reliable data

Records are complete and consistent enough to carry a flag.

4. Independent review

Reviewers and critics sit outside the body being examined.

5. Privacy protection

Public records only, no unnecessary personal data, stored as the law requires.

6. Safeguards against political misuse

The tool cannot be turned against rivals or critics.

AI can improve the identification of suspicious patterns, but it cannot independently establish guilt or replace institutional accountability. Corruption persists mainly through incentives, power and the willingness to act, not missing data. The verdict flips to a firm no if the computational, labour and governance costs outweigh the accountability gains, or if any of the six conditions cannot be met. The bill shows the costs are manageable only because the design is deliberately small, public-data-only and human-verified.

AI transparency statement

What AI contributed, what was accepted, and what was rejected.

The author used AI tools, including ChatGPT and Claude, for research organisation, brainstorming, web searches for fact-checking, drafting connecting prose and building the website. The research question, the argument that corruption is about incentives and political will, the choice of Kazakhstan procurement, and the verdict conditions are the author’s. Claude suggested the theory sources (Persson et al.; North; Nurkey et al.) and the DOZORRO case, and these were checked against publisher or catalogue records.

Accepted: narrowing the scope to public procurement, representing Palaces as institutions, using Brazil’s ALICE and Ukraine’s DOZORRO as case studies, and adding human verification with a right of reply.

Rejected: treating automated alerts as proof of wrongdoing. Excluded: statistics and a 2026 article cited by one AI tool that could not be traced to a source, and a public whistleblower submission feature, because of its ethical and legal risks.

Whistle Stop presents AI as a supporting instrument for public accountability, not as an autonomous authority that can determine corruption.
